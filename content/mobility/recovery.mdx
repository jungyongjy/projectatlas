Recovery mobility is distinct from training mobility. The goal is not to improve range of motion or strengthen tissues — it is to promote blood flow, reduce stiffness, and support the body's natural recovery processes. Recovery mobility should leave you feeling better, not fatigued.

## Philosophy

Recovery mobility follows three rules:

1. **Gentle** — Never push into pain or discomfort
2. **Non-fatiguing** — Zero resistance, zero load, zero intensity
3. **Enjoyable** — The session should feel good; if it does not, do less

<InfoBox title="Recovery mobility is optional">
Unlike pre-training warm-ups (which are essential), recovery mobility is a nice-to-have. On days when you feel good and have time, it supports recovery. On days when rest is the priority, skip it without guilt. Sleep is a more powerful recovery tool than any mobility routine.
</InfoBox>

## The Recovery Routine

Total time: 10–15 minutes. Perform on rest days or after training if desired.

### 1. Cat-Cow (Spinal Flexion/Extension)

| Parameter | Guideline |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Position** | All-fours |
| **Movement** | Slowly alternate between spinal flexion (cat) and extension (cow) |
| **Reps** | 8–10, moving with breath |

### 2. Child's Pose with Lateral Reach

| Parameter | Guideline |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Position** | Kneeling, hips to heels, arms extended forward |
| **Movement** | Walk hands to each side, feeling a gentle stretch through the lats and upper back |
| **Hold** | 30 seconds centre, 30 seconds each side |

### 3. Supine Twist

| Parameter | Guideline |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Position** | Lying on back, arms out to sides |
| **Movement** | Drop both knees to one side, keeping shoulders on the ground |
| **Hold** | 45–60 seconds per side |

### 4. Legs Up the Wall

| Parameter | Guideline |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Position** | Lying on back, legs resting vertically against a wall |
| **Movement** | Relax completely; arms at sides or on belly |
| **Hold** | 2–5 minutes |

This position promotes venous return from the legs — particularly beneficial after a long run or a day with high step count.

### 5. Deep Breathing (Diaphragmatic)

| Parameter | Guideline |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Position** | Lying on back, one hand on chest, one on belly |
| **Movement** | Breathe so that only the belly hand rises; chest hand stays still |
| **Duration** | 2–3 minutes |

<CoachNote>
Legs up the wall is the single highest-ROI recovery position. It requires zero effort, promotes circulatory recovery in the legs, and forces stillness — which is itself a skill worth developing. Two to five minutes, no phone, no music. Just breathe.
</CoachNote>

## Post-Training Cool-Down

After running or gym sessions, a brief cool-down supports the transition from training to recovery.

| Activity | Duration |
|----------|----------|
| Light walking or very easy jogging | 3–5 minutes |
| Static stretching (if desired) | 30 seconds per tight area |
| Hydration | Within 15 minutes of finishing |

<InfoBox title="Static stretching after training">
Unlike pre-training (where static stretching reduces performance), post-training static stretching is appropriate. Muscles are warm and pliable. This is the time to address specific areas of tightness with 30–60 second holds.
</InfoBox>

## What Recovery Mobility Is Not

| It is not... | Explanation |
|-------------|------------|
| A workout | If you are breathing hard or sweating, it is not recovery |
| A flexibility session | Aggressive stretching creates stress, not recovery |
| Foam rolling aggressively | Gentle rolling is fine; aggressive rolling on sore tissues creates additional damage |
| Mandatory | Some days, lying on the couch is the best recovery modality |

<TipBox>
The best recovery modality is the one you actually do. If legs up the wall feels like a chore, do something else. A gentle walk, a few minutes of cat-cow, or simply lying still with deep breathing all support recovery. The specific exercise matters far less than the intention: rest, gently.
</TipBox>

<ScientificNote>
Active recovery (gentle movement between training sessions) modestly improves clearance of metabolic byproducts and reduces delayed-onset muscle soreness compared to complete rest. However, the effect size is small — roughly 5–10% improvement in recovery markers. The primary benefit of recovery mobility may be psychological: the ritual of doing something kind for the body reinforces the identity of someone who takes recovery seriously. Sleep, nutrition, and stress management remain the dominant recovery variables.
</ScientificNote>
