Interval training is the most potent stimulus for improving VO₂max and running speed. Six 400-metre repeats at high intensity with recovery periods develop the physiological systems that determine how fast you can run.

<Figure name="intervals" caption="Six by 400 metre interval pattern on a track" />

## Purpose

400 m intervals develop:

- VO₂max (maximal oxygen uptake)
- Running economy at high speeds
- Anaerobic capacity
- Neuromuscular coordination
- Mental tolerance for high-intensity effort
- Stride power and turnover

## Execution

| Parameter | Guideline |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Repetitions** | 6 × 400 m |
| **Pace** | ~4:00–4:15/km (estimated) |
| **Recovery** | 90–120 seconds walking or slow jogging |
| **RPE (during rep)** | 8–9 out of 10 |
| **Total Session** | ~30 minutes |

### Session Structure

1. **Warm-up**: 10 minutes easy jogging + leg swings, high knees, dynamic hamstring swings
2. **6 × 400 m**: Each rep at target pace, walking/jogging recovery between reps
3. **Cool-down**: 10 minutes easy jogging

<CoachNote>
The quality of the last rep is the most important data point. If rep 6 is significantly slower than rep 1, you started too fast. The goal is six consistent efforts. Pacing discipline separates effective interval training from ego-driven burnout.
</CoachNote>

## Pacing

400 m intervals should be run at roughly 90–95% of maximum effort, fast, controlled, and repeatable.

| If your 2.4 km pace is... | Target 400 m pace |
|---------------------------|-------------------|
| 5:00/km | ~4:15–4:20/km |
| 4:52/km (current) | ~4:05–4:15/km |
| 4:30/km | ~3:50–4:00/km |
| 4:22/km (target) | ~3:45–3:55/km |

<InfoBox title="Pace is a guide, not a rule">
These paces are estimates. Adjust based on how you feel. If you cannot complete six reps at the target pace, slow down, completing the workout at a slightly slower pace is better than failing after three reps.
</InfoBox>

## Benefits

400 m intervals provide multiple adaptations that directly improve 2.4 km performance:

- **Increased VO₂max**: The primary determinant of endurance performance
- **Improved running economy**: Less energy required at any given pace
- **Enhanced buffering capacity**: Better tolerance of metabolic acidosis
- **Neuromuscular power**: Stronger, more efficient stride
- **Mental conditioning**: Confidence at high intensities

## Progression

Progress intervals conservatively. Speed work carries the highest injury risk of any running session.

| Stage | Progression |
|-------|------------|
| **Weeks 1–4** | Establish consistent pacing across all 6 reps |
| **Weeks 5–8** | Slightly increase pace (2–3 seconds per 400 m) |
| **Weeks 9–12** | Add 1–2 additional reps (7–8 × 400 m) |

<WarningBox title="Do not progress volume and pace simultaneously">
Increasing pace and adding repetitions in the same training block multiplies injury risk. Pick one variable, progress it for 4 weeks, then reassess.
</WarningBox>

## Common Mistakes

### Starting Too Fast

Rep 1 at nearly sprint pace, rep 6 at jogging pace. This turns a quality interval session into a failed workout. The adaptation comes from consistent effort, not from how fast one rep was.

### Insufficient Recovery Between Reps

Walking recovery is not wasted time. It allows the next rep to be performed at the target intensity. Cutting recovery short degrades rep quality without providing additional adaptation.

### Skipping the Warm-Up

Cold muscles at high intensity is a reliable formula for injury. The warm-up is not optional.

### Performing Intervals When Fatigued

If sleep was poor or legs are heavy from a hard gym session, consider postponing intervals. High-intensity work performed in a fatigued state increases injury risk without providing meaningful adaptation.

<ScientificNote>
Interval training at ~90–95% VO₂max stimulates central adaptations (increased stroke volume, cardiac output) and peripheral adaptations (mitochondrial enzyme activity, capillary density) that are difficult to achieve through steady-state training alone. The 400 m distance is particularly effective because it allows sufficient time at VO₂max without the excessive fatigue of longer intervals.
</ScientificNote>
