## General

### Why only three runs per week?

Three sessions is the minimum effective dose for consistent aerobic improvement while allowing three strength sessions and adequate recovery. Four running days would compromise gym performance and increase overuse injury risk without proportional benefit for a hybrid athlete whose primary goal is 2.4 km performance.

### Can I run more than three times per week?

Not within the current programme structure. Adding a fourth run would require reducing gym volume or frequency, which would compromise strength and physique goals. If your goals shift toward endurance (e.g., half marathon), the programme would need restructuring, but that is not the current objective.

### What if I miss a run?

Continue the schedule. Do not attempt to "make up" missed sessions by doubling up the following day or adding a run on a rest day. One missed run is negligible over a training year. The reaction to a missed session (panic, overcompensation) causes more harm than the missed session itself.

<CoachNote>
Missed sessions are normal. Life happens. The athletes who succeed long-term are not the ones who never miss, they are the ones who miss a session and calmly resume the programme the next day.
</CoachNote>

## Zone 2

### How do I know if I am actually in Zone 2?

Use the talk test: can you speak in complete sentences without gasping? If yes, you are in Zone 2. Heart rate (~140–150 bpm) is a secondary confirmation. Pace is the least reliable indicator; it varies with sleep, stress, weather, and fatigue.

### What if my heart rate is high but I feel fine?

On some days (poor sleep, stress, heat, caffeine) heart rate will be elevated at the same perceived effort. This is normal. Trust perceived effort over heart rate when they disagree. The adaptation comes from the effort level, not from a specific heart rate number.

### Why is Zone 2 pace improving so slowly?

Zone 2 improvement is measured in months, not weeks. Mitochondrial biogenesis and capillary development are slow processes. If your Zone 2 pace has improved by 5–10 seconds per kilometre over 3–4 months, that is excellent progress.

## Intervals

### Should I run intervals on a track or on the road?

A track is ideal because it provides an accurately measured 400 m distance and a predictable, even surface. Road intervals are acceptable if a track is unavailable, use a measured stretch of flat road and focus on time-based intervals (e.g., 90 seconds hard) rather than distance-based.

### What if I cannot complete all six reps?

Slow down. Completing six consistent reps at a slightly slower pace is vastly better than three perfect reps followed by three failed ones. Progress comes from completing the workout as designed, not from how fast one rep was.

### Can I do intervals on tired legs?

No. Intervals demand freshness to be effective and safe. If your legs are heavy from the previous day's session, swap Tuesday's hard run for an easy Zone 2 run and do intervals the following week.

## Threshold

### How is threshold different from intervals?

Threshold is a **continuous** effort at a pace you can sustain for approximately 20–40 minutes. Intervals are **repeated** efforts with recovery between. Threshold improves sustained lactate clearance; intervals improve peak speed and VO₂max. Both are necessary.

### Can I listen to music during threshold runs?

Yes, but be aware that music can mask effort signals. For the first few threshold sessions, consider running without music to develop a better sense of pacing and effort. Once pacing is consistent, music can help with the psychological demands of sustained discomfort.

## Long Run

### What if I cannot run 10 km?

Run the distance you can complete while remaining conversational. If that is 7 km today, it will be 8 km in a month and 10 km in a few months. The distance matters less than the consistency, a 7 km long run every Saturday for a year is far more valuable than sporadic 10 km efforts.

### Should my long run pace be slower than my Thursday Zone 2?

Yes, slightly. The long run is about duration, not pace. Starting slower than your usual Zone 2 pace helps ensure you finish the distance without fatigue accumulation that affects the following week's training.

## Concurrent Training

### Does lifting hurt my running?

Done correctly, no. Strength training supports running by improving running economy, reducing injury risk, and maintaining muscle mass. The interference effect (strength training reducing endurance adaptation) is real but manageable, the Atlas programme structure is designed to minimise it through scheduling and volume control.

### Should I run before or after lifting?

If performed on the same day (not recommended in the current schedule), run first. Running on pre-fatigued legs from lifting reinforces poor mechanics and increases injury risk. Lifting after running is acceptable but may reduce strength output. The Atlas schedule separates running and lifting by at least one sleep cycle.

## General Troubleshooting

### Running feels consistently harder than usual

Check, in order: sleep quality, hydration, protein intake, stress levels, and whether you are due for a deload. If all are adequate and the feeling persists for more than two weeks, reduce running volume by 20–30% for one week and reassess.

### I am not seeing pace improvements

Pace improvement at the 2.4 km distance comes slowly. Ensure you are:

- Completing all three weekly runs consistently
- Running Zone 2 at actual Zone 2 effort (not moderate)
- Performing quality sessions at the prescribed intensities
- Sleeping 7–8 hours consistently
- Eating adequate protein and calories

If all of the above are true for 8+ weeks and pace has not improved, the programme may need adjustment, but verify the basics first.

<CoachNote>
Most "programming problems" are actually consistency, recovery, or nutrition problems. Before changing the programme, verify that the programme is actually being followed.
</CoachNote>
