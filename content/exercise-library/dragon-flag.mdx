The Dragon Flag is an advanced core exercise that develops anterior core strength through a full range of motion. Unlike crunches (which flex the spine) or planks (which hold a static position), the Dragon Flag challenges the core to resist extension while the lower body moves through space — a more athletic and transferable demand.

## Purpose

Rectus abdominis strength and hypertrophy. Anti-extension core control — the ability to prevent the lower back from arching while the legs move. This is the core's primary athletic function.

## Muscles

| Role | Muscles |
|------|---------|
| **Primary** | Rectus abdominis, hip flexors (iliopsoas, rectus femoris) |
| **Secondary** | Obliques (stabilising), serratus anterior, latissimus dorsi (gripping the bench) |

## Execution

1. Lie on a flat bench, gripping the bench behind the head
2. Lift the body so only the upper back and shoulders remain on the bench
3. Keeping the body in a straight line from shoulders to feet, lower under control
4. Lower as far as possible without the lower back arching or the hips dropping
5. Return to the top position

| Parameter | Guideline |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Sets × Reps** | 3 × As many as possible with good form |
| **Range** | Full — body straight at top, lower as far as core control allows |
| **Progression** | Start with bent knees (easier); progress to straight legs, then one leg at a time |

<CoachNote>
The Dragon Flag is an advanced exercise. If you cannot perform a full repetition, begin with negatives: jump to the top position and lower as slowly as possible. Build to 3–5 controlled negatives before attempting full reps. Bent-knee Dragon Flags are an intermediate step. No one performs these perfectly on day one.
</CoachNote>

| **Running** | ★★★☆☆ | Core anti-extension strength supports running posture and force transfer between upper and lower body |
| **Strength** | ★★★☆☆ | Core stability supports heavier loading in compound lifts |
| **Athleticism** | ★★★★☆ | Direct transfer — resisting extension while the limbs move is the core's primary athletic function |

## Common Mistakes

### Arching the Lower Back

The lower back arches as the legs lower — this is the failure point the exercise is designed to strengthen. Reduce the range of motion: only lower as far as you can maintain a neutral spine. Over weeks, this range will increase.

### Bending at the Hips

The body should remain in a straight line. Bending at the hips (legs drop while torso stays elevated) turns the exercise into a partial leg lower. Keep the whole body rigid.
