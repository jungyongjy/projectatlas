## Overview

The Pallof Press is an anti-rotation core exercise: the core resists a rotational force while the arms move. This is precisely the core's function during running, where the upper and lower body rotate in opposite directions with each stride and the core's job is to control and transfer force between them without collapsing.

## Purpose

Anti-rotation core strength. Oblique and deep spinal stabiliser development. Direct transfer to running, since resisting unwanted rotation is what the core does during gait.

## Execution

1. Attach a single handle to a cable pulley set at approximately chest height
2. Stand sideways to the cable, gripping the handle with both hands at the centre of the chest
3. Step away from the machine to create tension; the cable should be trying to rotate you toward the machine
4. Press the handle straight out in front of the chest, resisting the rotational pull
5. Hold at full extension for 1–2 seconds
6. Return the handle to the chest under control

| Parameter | Guideline |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Sets × Reps** | 2 × 10 per side |
| **Hold** | 1–2 seconds at full extension |
| **Stance** | Feet shoulder-width, knees slightly bent, hips and shoulders square |

## Common Mistakes

### Rotating Toward the Machine

Allowing the torso to rotate, the very thing the exercise is designed to prevent. Reduce the weight until you can maintain square hips and shoulders throughout the movement.

### Narrow Stance

A narrow stance reduces stability. Widen the feet to shoulder-width or slightly wider, with a slight knee bend. Stability comes from the ground up.

### Holding Breath

The deep core muscles (transversus abdominis, pelvic floor) are coordinated with breathing. Holding breath creates intra-abdominal pressure but limits the specific anti-rotation training effect. Breathe normally throughout the movement.

## Running Transfer

**Transfer: ★★★★★.** Anti-rotation is the core's primary running function. During gait, the pelvis and thorax rotate in opposite directions, and the obliques and deep stabilisers must resist unwanted rotation while transferring force. The Pallof Press trains exactly this quality with minimal fatigue.

<CoachNote>
If you feel the Pallof Press primarily in your arms or shoulders, you are using too much weight or your stance is too narrow. The exercise should be felt in the obliques and deep core. Widen your stance, bend your knees slightly, and focus on resisting the rotation rather than on pressing the handle. The press is secondary; the anti-rotation is the point.
</CoachNote>

<ScientificNote>
Core training that emphasises anti-rotation has been shown to transfer more directly to athletic performance than traditional flexion-based abdominal work. In running, the core's primary role is to control rotation and transfer force between the upper and lower body, a demand the Pallof Press replicates with low injury risk.
</ScientificNote>
