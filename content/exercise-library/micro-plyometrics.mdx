Micro Plyometrics are brief, low-volume plyometric exercises performed at the end of each gym session. They are not a conditioning workout — they are a neuromuscular stimulus designed to improve tendon stiffness and reactive strength with minimal fatigue.

## Purpose

Tendon stiffness and elastic energy return. Reactive strength — the ability to produce force quickly upon ground contact. Running economy improvement. The dose is deliberately small: two sets of twenty repetitions, performed fresh after calf raises.

## Exercises

### Pogo Jumps

| Parameter | Guideline |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Position** | Standing, feet hip-width, hands on hips |
| **Movement** | Small, rapid bounces — minimal ground contact time, minimal knee bend |
| **Cue** | "Hot coals" — the feet should spend as little time on the ground as possible |
| **Sets × Reps** | 2 × 20 |

### Ankle Hops

| Parameter | Guideline |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Position** | Standing, feet hip-width |
| **Movement** | Small hops using only ankle motion — knees remain nearly straight |
| **Cue** | "Stiff springs" — minimal ground contact, all movement from the ankles |
| **Sets × Reps** | 2 × 20 |

<CoachNote>
If you are fatiguing during plyometrics, you are doing them wrong. The goal is maximal quality with minimal fatigue. If ground contact time is increasing or jumps are losing height, you have done enough — stop, even if you have not completed the prescribed reps. Quality over quantity.
</CoachNote>

| Domain | Rating | Notes |
|--------|--------|-------|
| **Running** | ★★★★★ | Direct transfer — plyometric training improves running economy by 2–8% in trained runners through improved musculotendinous stiffness |
| **Strength** | ★★☆☆☆ | Minimal direct strength carryover; complements heavy strength work |
| **Athleticism** | ★★★★★ | Reactive strength and tendon stiffness transfer to sprinting, jumping, and all explosive movement |

## Why So Little?

The dose is deliberately small for three reasons:

1. **Plyometrics are potent** — A small dose produces meaningful adaptation. More is not better.
2. **Fatigue must be minimal** — These are performed at the end of gym sessions, after the main work. They should not create fatigue that affects the next day's training.
3. **Concurrent training recovery** — With three runs and three gym sessions per week, recovery capacity is finite. Low-volume plyometrics provide the benefit without consuming significant recovery resources.

<ScientificNote>
Plyometric training improves running economy primarily through increased musculotendinous stiffness — the ability of tendons and connective tissue to store and return elastic energy during ground contact. This adaptation is specific to brief, high-force movements and is difficult to achieve through strength training alone. The combination of heavy resistance training (for force production capacity) and low-volume plyometrics (for rate of force development and tendon stiffness) produces superior running economy improvements compared to either modality in isolation. Notably, the effective dose is small — studies demonstrating significant running economy improvements from plyometrics typically use 60–200 foot contacts per session, 2–3 times per week. The Atlas prescription of ~80 contacts per session (2×20) performed 3 times per week falls within this evidence-based range.
</ScientificNote>

## Common Mistakes

### Too Many Repetitions

More plyometrics is not better. Additional volume increases fatigue without proportionally increasing adaptation. The dose-response curve flattens quickly — 2×20 provides most of the benefit.

### Poor Quality

Plyometrics performed with fatigue-induced poor mechanics reinforce poor movement patterns and increase injury risk. If quality degrades, stop — regardless of how many reps remain.

### Skipping Them

Because plyometrics are brief and placed at the end of the session, they are easy to skip. This is a mistake — they provide a unique stimulus (tendon stiffness) that no other exercise in the programme replicates.
