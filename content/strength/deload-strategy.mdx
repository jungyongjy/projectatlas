A deload is a planned period of reduced training stress. It is not a week off. It is not a sign of weakness. It is a programmed component of the training cycle, as essential as the hardest quality session.

## Why Deload?

Training creates two things: fitness and fatigue. Fitness accumulates slowly. Fatigue accumulates faster. Over weeks, fatigue builds to a point where it masks fitness, you are stronger and fitter than you appear, but fatigue prevents you from expressing it.

A deload clears fatigue while preserving fitness. The result: you emerge stronger, not weaker.

<InfoBox title="Deloads reveal progress, not reverse it">
The strength you express in the gym is fitness minus fatigue. When fatigue is high, performance drops, not because you are weaker, but because you are tired. A deload removes the fatigue component, revealing the fitness that was there all along.
</InfoBox>

## When to Deload

### Scheduled (Recommended)

Every **8–10 weeks**, regardless of how you feel. Scheduled deloads prevent excessive fatigue accumulation before it becomes a problem.

### Reactive (If Needed)

Sooner than 8 weeks if:

- Performance declines across multiple sessions
- Motivation is consistently low
- Sleep quality has deteriorated
- Joints or connective tissues feel persistently sore
- Session quality is declining despite adequate sleep and nutrition

<CoachNote>
If you wait until you "need" a deload, you have already waited too long. Scheduled deloads are preventive maintenance. Reactive deloads are repairs. Prevention is cheaper than repair, in training as in everything else.
</CoachNote>

## How to Deload

### Volume Reduction

Reduce training volume by approximately **50%**. This can be achieved through:

| Method | Example |
|--------|---------|
| **Fewer sets** | 1–2 sets per exercise instead of 3 |
| **Fewer reps** | Stay at the bottom of the rep range |
| **Lighter load** | Reduce weight by 10–20% |

### What to Maintain

| Maintain | Why |
|----------|-----|
| **Frequency** | Still train 3× per week: do not skip sessions |
| **Exercise selection** | Keep the same exercises: do not introduce new movements |
| **Movement quality** | Maintain tempo and range of motion |
| **Running schedule** | Deload applies to the gym, not running (unless running also needs reduction) |

### What to Reduce

| Reduce | How |
|--------|-----|
| **Volume** | 3 sets → 1–2 sets per exercise |
| **Intensity** | Reduce weight by 10–20% |
| **Total session time** | Should be ~15–20 minutes |

### Example Deload Session

| Exercise | Normal | Deload |
|----------|--------|--------|
| Leg Press | 3 × 8–12 @ 120 kg | 2 × 8 @ 100 kg |
| Leg Curl | 3 × 8–12 | 2 × 8 |
| Shoulder Press | 3 × 8–12 | 2 × 8 |
| Pull-Ups | 3 × max | 2 × submaximal |

Continue through the template at reduced volume. The session should feel almost too easy; that is the point.

<ScientificNote>
Research on tapering and deloading consistently demonstrates that training volume can be reduced by 40–60% for 1–2 weeks without performance decrement, and often with performance improvement due to fatigue dissipation. Intensity (load on the bar) can be maintained or slightly reduced without losing adaptation. The key is maintaining training frequency, the body retains the neural pattern even when volume and load are reduced.
</ScientificNote>

## Deload and Running

The gym deload does not automatically apply to running. Running volume and intensity can be maintained during a strength deload.

However, if **both** strength and running performance have declined, consider a concurrent deload:

| Modality | Action |
|----------|--------|
| **Gym** | Reduce volume by 50% |
| **Running** | Skip Tuesday's Hard Run; replace with easy Zone 2 |
| **Long Run** | Reduce to 5–7 km at easy pace |

<WarningBox title="A deload is not a week off">
Skipping all training for a week causes detraining, not recovery. The goal is reduced stress, not zero stress. Train, but train lightly. The stimulus must remain for fitness to be preserved.
</WarningBox>

## After the Deload

Return to normal programming gradually:

| Week | Volume |
|------|--------|
| **Deload week** | 50% |
| **Week after** | 80–90% (slight ramp-up) |
| **Week 2 after** | 100% (normal programming) |

Do not attempt to "make up for lost time" by training harder after a deload. The deload worked, you are fresher, stronger, and ready to resume progression. Trust the process.

<TipBox>
The first session after a deload may feel slightly off, coordination takes a session to return. The second session should feel significantly better. By the third session, you should feel stronger than before the deload. If not, recovery fundamentals (sleep, nutrition, stress) need attention.
</TipBox>
