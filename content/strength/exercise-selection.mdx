Every exercise in the Atlas programme has been selected against five criteria. Exercises that fail to satisfy these criteria are replaced. This page documents the rationale behind each selection.

## Selection Criteria

| Criterion | Question |
|-----------|----------|
| **High Stimulus** | Does it produce meaningful adaptation? |
| **Low Fatigue** | Is the recovery cost proportionate to the benefit? |
| **Easy Progression** | Can it be overloaded simply and reliably? |
| **Safe** | Is the injury risk acceptably low? |
| **Practical** | Is it available in a commercial gym? |

<InfoBox title="No exercise is permanent">
Exercise selection is reviewed periodically. If a better option exists — higher stimulus, lower fatigue, safer, more practical — the programme should change. But changes should be rare, deliberate, and evidence-informed, not impulsive.
</InfoBox>

## Compound Movements

### Leg Press
**Why it was selected**: Quad-dominant compound with minimal axial fatigue. Heavier loading than Bulgarian Split Squats without the balance demand. Easy to progress (pin-loaded or plate-loaded).

**What it replaced**: Barbell Back Squat (disliked, high fatigue, high technical demand, high injury risk for this athlete).

**Transfer**: Moderate running transfer through quad and glute strength. Low direct transfer compared to unilateral work.

### Bulgarian Split Squat
**Why it was selected**: Unilateral quad and glute development with hip stability and balance demands. Direct transfer to running through single-leg strength. Addresses unilateral weaknesses that bilateral exercises mask.

**What it replaced**: Leg Extensions (effective quad isolation but poor overall ROI — no stability demand, no glute contribution, no athletic transfer).

**Transfer**: High running transfer through unilateral stability, quad strength, and hip control.

### Machine Shoulder Press
**Why it was selected**: Stable overhead pressing without the technical demand of a barbell. Develops anterior and medial deltoids, triceps. Easy to progress with pin-loaded increments.

**Transfer**: Moderate — shoulder strength supports arm drive during running. Not a primary performance driver.

### Chest Press
**Why it was selected**: Horizontal pressing for chest, anterior deltoid, and triceps development. Machine variant chosen for stability, safety, and ease of progression.

**Transfer**: Low direct running transfer. Supports upper body muscularity and balanced physique.

### Romanian Deadlift
**Why it was selected**: Posterior chain development (hamstrings, glutes, erectors) with lower fatigue than conventional deadlifts. No floor pull — reduced lower back stress. Direct hamstring and glute hypertrophy.

**What it replaced**: Conventional Deadlift (high fatigue, high lower back stress, disliked by athlete).

**Transfer**: Moderate running transfer through posterior chain strength. Hamstring strength supports running propulsion.

<CoachNote>
The Romanian Deadlift is the most technically demanding exercise in the programme. Prioritise hamstring stretch and control over load. A perfectly executed set at a moderate weight produces better results than a heavy set with rounded lumbar spine.
</CoachNote>

## Pulling Movements

### Pull-Ups
**Why it was selected**: The highest-ROI vertical pull. Develops lats, biceps, grip, and core stability. Bodyweight provides automatic load regulation.

**Transfer**: Moderate — upper back strength supports running posture. Grip strength transfers to daily life and other lifts.

### Cable Row
**Why it was selected**: Horizontal pull for mid-back thickness and scapular control. Cable provides constant tension throughout the range of motion.

**Transfer**: Moderate — scapular retraction and thoracic posture support running form, especially during fatigue.

### Face Pull
**Why it was selected**: Rear delt and external rotator development. Critical for shoulder health and posture — counterbalances the internal rotation bias of pressing and desk work.

**Transfer**: Indirect but important — shoulder health enables consistent training. Postural benefits transfer to daily life.

### Reverse Pec Deck
**Why it was selected**: Rear delt and upper back development. Machine provides stable, controlled movement. Complements Face Pull for complete posterior shoulder development.

**Transfer**: Same rationale as Face Pull — shoulder health as an enabler of consistent training.

## Direct Arm Work

### Preacher Curl
**Why it was selected**: Biceps isolation with strict form enforced by the preacher bench. Eliminates momentum. High stimulus-to-fatigue ratio.

**Transfer**: Minimal direct running transfer. Maintains upper body muscularity — important for physique goals.

### Hammer Curl
**Why it was selected**: Brachioradialis and biceps development with a neutral grip (reduced wrist stress). Complements Preacher Curl for complete arm development.

**Transfer**: Same rationale as Preacher Curl.

### Cable Triceps Extension
**Why it was selected**: Triceps isolation with constant cable tension. High stimulus-to-fatigue ratio. Supports pressing strength and arm appearance. Included in both templates for adequate weekly volume.

**Transfer**: Moderate — triceps contribute to pressing movements. Arm appearance supports physique goals.

<InfoBox title="Why direct arm work is retained">
Arms contribute significantly to overall physique. Removing direct arm work would provide minimal recovery benefit (arms recover quickly) while noticeably reducing hypertrophy stimulus. The ROI calculation favours retention — approximately six direct sets each for biceps and triceps per week.
</InfoBox>

### Lateral Raise
**Why it was selected**: Medial deltoid isolation — the only effective way to develop shoulder width. High stimulus-to-fatigue ratio. Included in both templates for adequate frequency.

**Transfer**: Low direct running transfer. High physique transfer — shoulder width significantly affects upper body appearance.

## Core and Hip Work

### Dragon Flag
**Why it was selected**: Advanced anti-extension core exercise. Develops rectus abdominis and hip flexor control through a full range of motion. Scalable by adjusting lever length.

**Transfer**: Moderate — core stability underpins running posture and force transfer between upper and lower body.

### Pallof Press
**Why it was selected**: Anti-rotation core exercise. Develops the obliques and deep spinal stabilisers in a movement pattern that resists unwanted rotation — directly applicable to running.

**Transfer**: High — resisting rotation while the limbs move is precisely what the core does during running.

### Hip Thrust
**Why it was selected**: Direct glute isolation with high loading potential. Glute strength and hypertrophy with minimal fatigue. Supports running propulsion and hip extension.

**What it replaced**: Back Extensions (lower glute activation, higher lower back involvement).

**Transfer**: High running transfer through glute strength — glutes are the primary hip extensor during running.

### Hip Abductor / Adductor
**Why they were selected**: Direct hip abductor and adductor development. These muscles stabilise the pelvis during running and are often undertrained. Machine provides controlled, isolated loading.

**Transfer**: Moderate — pelvic stability during running stance phase. Hip abductor weakness is associated with several running-related injuries.

## Calves

### Standing Calf Raise
**Why it was selected**: The primary calf developer. Standing position trains the gastrocnemius through a full range of motion. Calves are critical for running — they handle forces several times bodyweight with each step.

**Transfer**: Very high — calf strength and stiffness directly affect running economy. The calf-Achilles complex is the primary spring during running.

## Plyometrics

### Micro Plyometrics (Pogo Jumps / Ankle Hops)
**Why they were selected**: Low-volume, low-fatigue plyometric work to develop tendon stiffness, reactive strength, and running economy. Two sets of twenty reps produces meaningful adaptation with negligible fatigue.

**Transfer**: Very high — plyometric training improves running economy by 2–8% in trained runners through improved musculotendinous stiffness and force return.

<ScientificNote>
Plyometric training improves running economy primarily through increased musculotendinous stiffness — the ability of tendons and muscles to store and return elastic energy during ground contact. This adaptation is specific to brief, high-force movements (like hopping and jumping) and is difficult to achieve through strength training alone. The combination of heavy strength work (for force production) and light plyometrics (for stiffness and rate of force development) produces superior running economy improvements compared to either modality alone.
</ScientificNote>
