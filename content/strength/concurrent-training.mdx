Concurrent training (developing strength and endurance simultaneously) is the defining challenge of hybrid athletic development. The body adapts to both stimuli, but the signals can conflict. Managing this conflict is the central programming problem Atlas solves.

## The Interference Effect

When strength and endurance training are combined, each can blunt the other's adaptation. This is not a reason to avoid concurrent training, it is a reason to programme it intelligently.

### What causes interference?

| Factor | Effect |
|--------|--------|
| **Residual fatigue** | Endurance training depletes glycogen and creates muscular fatigue that reduces strength output |
| **Conflicting signalling** | Endurance training activates AMPK (promoting endurance adaptation); strength training activates mTOR (promoting hypertrophy). These pathways can inhibit each other |
| **Recovery competition** | Both modalities require recovery resources. When combined, recovery capacity is shared: not additive |
| **Caloric partitioning** | The body must decide whether to allocate resources toward endurance adaptation or strength adaptation |

<ScientificNote>
The molecular interference between AMPK and mTOR signalling is real but often overstated in popular fitness literature. Practical interference (accumulated fatigue preventing quality strength sessions) is a far more significant concern for most athletes than molecular-level signalling conflicts. Programme around fatigue, and the molecular biology largely takes care of itself.
</ScientificNote>

## How Atlas Manages Interference

### Scheduling Separation

Hard sessions of different modalities are never placed on consecutive days.

| Day | Session | Interference Risk |
|-----|---------|-------------------|
| Monday | Gym | Low (followed by easy run) |
| Tuesday | Zone 2 | Very low (easy effort) |
| Wednesday | Gym | Low (followed by rest) |
| Thursday | Rest | None |
| Friday | Quality Run | Low (followed by upper-body gym) |
| Saturday | Gym | Low–Moderate (followed by easy long run) |
| Sunday | Long Run | Low (followed by gym after sleep) |

### Volume Control

Neither modality is trained at maximal volume:

- **Running**: 3 sessions (not 5–6)
- **Strength**: 3 sessions (not 4–5), ~30 minutes each
- **Lower body**: Moderate volume, programmed away from quality runs

### Exercise Selection

Strength exercises are chosen partly for their low interference with running:

- Machine compounds produce less systemic fatigue than barbell equivalents
- Unilateral work (Bulgarian Split Squats) develops strength without the axial loading of barbell squats
- Direct arm work is retained because it creates minimal interference

### Recovery Prioritisation

Recovery resources are finite. Atlas allocates them deliberately:

| Priority | Allocation |
|----------|-----------|
| 1 | Quality run performance (Friday) |
| 2 | Long run completion (Sunday) |
| 3 | Gym session quality (Monday, Wednesday, Saturday) |
| 4 | Zone 2 consistency (Tuesday) |

<CoachNote>
When recovery is limited (poor sleep, high stress, illness) preserve running quality first, then gym consistency. A slightly easier gym session has minimal long-term impact. A missed or poor-quality run has greater consequence for programme goals.
</CoachNote>

## Practical Guidelines

### Nutrition for Concurrent Training

- **Protein**: 140–160 g/day minimises muscle loss during endurance training
- **Carbohydrates**: Adequate intake around running sessions maintains glycogen
- **Calories**: Slight surplus or maintenance supports both adaptations
- **Timing**: Protein within 1–2 hours of gym sessions; carbohydrates within 1–2 hours of long runs

### Monitoring Interference

Signs that interference is excessive:

- Consistent strength regression (not just one bad session)
- Poor running performance despite adequate sleep
- Persistent heavy legs during Zone 2 runs
- Increased injury frequency
- Declining motivation for either modality

If two or more of these signs persist for 2+ weeks, reduce volume in one modality temporarily.

<WarningBox title="More is not better">
The solution to poor progress from concurrent training is almost never "add more training." It is usually "improve recovery" or "reduce volume in one modality." Adding work when recovery is already insufficient compounds the problem.
</WarningBox>

<TipBox>
Concurrent training is a long game. In any given week, one modality may feel stronger than the other. Over months, both should trend upward. Do not overreact to short-term fluctuations, they are normal.
</TipBox>
