Every programming decision in Atlas is evaluated against five principles. These principles are deliberately few — too many rules creates confusion, and confusion leads to inconsistency.

If a decision cannot be justified against at least one of these principles, it should be reconsidered.

## Principle 1 — Train for Decades

> Every decision should still make sense when viewed over a ten-year horizon.

Short-term thinking is the most common programming error. Aggressive progression, excessive volume, and constant programme changes all optimise the next four weeks at the expense of the next four years.

### Questions to ask

- Can I realistically sustain this?
- Will this still benefit me in five years?
- Does this increase or reduce long-term resilience?
- Would I recommend this to someone I care about?

### What this looks like in practice

- Conservative progression (not maximal)
- Exercise selection prioritising safety
- Deload weeks programmed in advance
- Mobility work maintained year-round
- Sleep and nutrition treated as training variables

<CoachNote>
The most impressive training programme is worthless if you cannot follow it. Consistency is the strongest predictor of long-term results — stronger than programme design, exercise selection, or supplementation.
</CoachNote>

## Principle 2 — The Hybrid Athlete

> Atlas develops multiple physical qualities simultaneously. A small compromise in each individual quality is acceptable if overall capability improves.

No single quality — strength, endurance, or physique — should dominate at the expense of the others.

### The six qualities

| Quality | Role |
|---------|------|
| **Strength** | Force production, injury prevention, bone density |
| **Hypertrophy** | Muscle maintenance, metabolic health, resilience |
| **Aerobic Capacity** | Work capacity, recovery, cardiovascular health |
| **Running Economy** | Speed without additional fitness |
| **Mobility** | Joint health, movement quality, stiffness reduction |
| **Athleticism** | Coordination, balance, power, control |

### What this rejects

- Marathon mileage (sacrifices muscle and strength)
- Powerlifting volume (sacrifices running and mobility)
- Bodybuilding isolation overload (sacrifices time and recovery)
- Constant high-intensity conditioning (sacrifices everything)

<InfoBox title="Hybrid does not mean average">
The goal is not mediocrity across six domains. It is meaningful competence across all six — good enough in each to create an athletic, capable, resilient body.
</InfoBox>

## Principle 3 — Minimum Effective Dose

> Maximise adaptation per unit of fatigue. Not per unit of volume.

More training does not equal better training. The objective is the smallest dose that produces meaningful adaptation.

### Every exercise must justify

- Its recovery cost
- Its time cost
- Its interference with running
- Its contribution toward long-term goals

### What this looks like in practice

- Three gym sessions, not five
- Three runs, not six
- ~30-minute strength sessions
- Low-volume plyometrics (2×20, not 5×20)
- Direct arm work retained (high ROI for physique)

<ScientificNote>
The dose-response relationship in training is not linear. Beyond the minimum effective dose, each additional unit of volume produces diminishing returns while recovery cost continues to rise. The optimal training dose is the smallest one that still drives adaptation — not the largest one you can tolerate.
</ScientificNote>

## Principle 4 — High Return on Investment

> Exercises should be selected based on value, not popularity.

High ROI exercises satisfy several conditions simultaneously.

### The five criteria

1. **High stimulus** — Produces meaningful adaptation
2. **Low fatigue** — Minimal recovery cost
3. **Easy progression** — Simple overload strategy
4. **Safe** — Low injury risk
5. **Practical** — Available in a commercial gym

### Examples

| High ROI | Low ROI (for this athlete) |
|----------|---------------------------|
| Bulgarian Split Squat | Barbell Back Squat |
| Machine Leg Press | Conventional Deadlift |
| Pull-Ups | Pendlay Row |
| Romanian Deadlift | Good Morning |
| Standing Calf Raise | Seated Calf Raise |

<CoachNote>
Exercise selection is not about "best" in absolute terms. It is about "best for this athlete, given their goals, constraints, and preferences." A theoretically superior exercise that you hate doing is worse than a slightly less optimal exercise you perform consistently.
</CoachNote>

## Principle 5 — Consistency Over Novelty

> Progression comes from improved execution, increased capacity, and gradual overload — not from exercise rotation.

The programme should change slowly. Exercises should not rotate simply because they become familiar.

### What this rejects

- "Muscle confusion" as a training principle
- Changing exercises every 4–6 weeks
- Chasing novelty over progressive overload
- Abandoning exercises that "feel stale"

### What drives actual progress

- Improved execution (technique, mind-muscle connection)
- Increased capacity (more reps at the same weight)
- Gradual overload (more weight for the same reps)
- Improved movement quality (mobility, stability, control)

<InfoBox title="Novelty is not progression">
A new exercise might feel more effective because it creates unfamiliar soreness. This is novelty, not adaptation. The exercises that have become "easy" are the ones you have mastered — they are the most productive tools in your arsenal.
</InfoBox>

## The Decision Framework

Whenever a training decision must be made, evaluate it against these five questions, in this order.

### Question 1

**Does it move me toward my long-term identity?**

If the answer is no, stop. Do not implement.

### Question 2

**Does it provide a high return on investment?**

If the stimulus-to-fatigue ratio is poor, reconsider.

### Question 3

**Can I recover from it?**

Recovery capacity is finite. Every addition consumes recovery that could be allocated elsewhere.

### Question 4

**Will it interfere with more important training?**

Running takes priority. Any strength or mobility change must not degrade running quality.

### Question 5

**Can I realistically continue doing this next year?**

Sustainability is the ultimate filter. Three questions from now, will this still be in the programme?

<WarningBox title="When to say no">
If the answer to any of these questions is "no," the decision should be rejected — regardless of how promising the intervention appears in research or how popular it is among athletes you respect.
</WarningBox>
