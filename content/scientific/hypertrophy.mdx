Hypertrophy — the increase in muscle size — is a central goal of the Atlas strength programme. Understanding the mechanisms allows efficient programming: maximum muscle retention with minimum time and fatigue investment.

## What Drives Hypertrophy?

Three primary mechanisms are supported by evidence:

### 1. Mechanical Tension

The primary driver of hypertrophy. Tension is created when muscles contract against resistance. Key variables:

- **Load**: 30–85% of one-rep maximum can produce hypertrophy, but 60–85% (roughly 8–15 RM) is most efficient per unit of fatigue
- **Range of motion**: Full range produces greater hypertrophy than partial range
- **Tempo**: Controlled eccentrics (2–3 seconds) enhance the hypertrophic stimulus

### 2. Metabolic Stress

The "pump" — accumulation of metabolites (lactate, hydrogen ions) during high-rep training. Contributes to hypertrophy through:

- Cell swelling (increased water content in muscle cells)
- Hormonal responses (growth hormone, IGF-1)
- Increased motor unit recruitment as fibres fatigue

### 3. Muscle Damage

Microscopic damage to muscle fibres triggers satellite cell activation and tissue remodelling. However, excessive damage impairs recovery without additional hypertrophy — the goal is moderate, not maximal, damage.

## Volume: How Much Is Enough?

The dose-response relationship between training volume and hypertrophy:

| Weekly Sets Per Muscle Group | Hypertrophy Response |
|------------------------------|---------------------|
| 1–4 | Some growth; suboptimal for most |
| 5–9 | Good response for most individuals |
| 10–20 | Near-maximal response |
| 20+ | Diminishing returns; recovery becomes limiting |

The Atlas programme provides approximately 6–12 direct weekly sets for most muscle groups — sufficient for maintenance and modest growth in an intermediate lifter.

<ScientificNote>
A 2017 meta-analysis by Schoenfeld et al. (Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research) found a clear dose-response relationship between weekly set volume and hypertrophy, with diminishing returns beyond approximately 10 weekly sets per muscle group. Importantly, these studies are conducted in non-endurance-training populations — the concurrent training demands of Atlas mean volume tolerance is lower than in pure strength athletes.
</ScientificNote>

## Hypertrophy and Endurance Training

Endurance training can be catabolic — particularly when:

- Caloric intake is inadequate
- Protein intake is insufficient
- Training volume is very high (marathon training levels)

Atlas manages this through moderate running volume (3 sessions), adequate protein (140–160 g), and consistent strength stimulus. The goal is muscle preservation while improving endurance — not maximal hypertrophy.
