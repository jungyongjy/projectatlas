Protein provides the amino acids required for muscle protein synthesis (MPS) — the process by which muscle tissue is repaired and rebuilt after training. Adequate protein intake is the single most important nutritional variable for a hybrid athlete.

## How Much Protein?

Protein requirements scale with training demands:

| Population | Recommendation (g/kg/day) |
|-----------|--------------------------|
| Sedentary adults | 0.8 |
| Endurance athletes | 1.2–1.6 |
| Strength athletes | 1.6–2.2 |
| **Concurrent athletes** | **1.6–2.2** (higher end for those in energy deficit or with high training volume) |

For an 80 kg athlete: 1.8–2.0 g/kg = 144–160 g/day.

<ScientificNote>
Protein requirements for concurrent athletes sit at the upper end of the range because: (1) endurance training increases amino acid oxidation, (2) strength training increases the threshold for maximal MPS stimulation, and (3) the combined volume of both modalities creates a higher total protein demand than either alone. A 2018 meta-analysis by Morton et al. (British Journal of Sports Medicine) found that protein intakes above 1.6 g/kg/day provided no additional lean mass gains in resistance-trained individuals — but this analysis did not include concurrent training populations, whose requirements may be slightly higher.
</ScientificNote>

## Distribution

MPS is stimulated by protein feeding but is refractory — it cannot be continuously maximised. Key findings:

- **Per-meal dose**: 0.4–0.55 g/kg (approximately 30–45 g for an 80 kg individual) maximises MPS
- **Spacing**: 3–5 hours between protein-containing meals allows MPS to return to baseline
- **Total intake dominates**: While distribution matters, total daily protein intake is the primary determinant of net protein balance

Practical implication: spread 140–160 g across 3–4 eating occasions. The current pattern (protein shake + lunch + dinner) with slightly larger portions achieves this.
