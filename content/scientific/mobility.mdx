Mobility — the ability to move through a range of motion with control — is distinct from flexibility (passive range of motion). The evidence on mobility training has evolved significantly over the past two decades, overturning several long-held beliefs.

## Stretching and Injury Prevention

The relationship between stretching and injury risk is not what was traditionally believed:

| Belief | Evidence |
|--------|----------|
| "Stretching before exercise prevents injury" | **Not supported.** Multiple large systematic reviews found no reduction in injury risk from pre-exercise stretching |
| "Static stretching before exercise reduces performance" | **Supported.** Static stretching reduces power output by 5–10% for up to 30 minutes |
| "Dynamic warm-up reduces injury risk" | **Modestly supported.** Dynamic warm-up improves performance and may modestly reduce injury risk through increased tissue temperature and neural activation |
| "Greater flexibility reduces injury risk" | **Not consistently supported.** Both very low and very high flexibility may increase injury risk in some populations |

<ScientificNote>
A 2014 systematic review by Lauersen et al. (British Journal of Sports Medicine) analysed 25 trials involving 26,610 participants and found that strength training reduced sports injuries to less than one-third, while stretching had no significant protective effect. Proprioception training also showed protective effects. The practical implication: strength training is the most evidence-supported injury prevention strategy — not stretching.
</ScientificNote>

## Mobility and Performance

| Question | Evidence |
|----------|----------|
| Does stretching improve running economy? | **No.** Several studies found no improvement; some found slight decrements |
| Does mobility training improve strength? | **Not directly.** Mobility enables positions from which force can be produced; it does not itself increase force production |
| Does joint-specific mobility affect injury risk? | **Possibly.** Ankle dorsiflexion limitation is associated with several running injuries; thoracic mobility affects shoulder health in overhead athletes |

## Practical Implications for Atlas

- **Dynamic warm-up before training** — supported by evidence for performance and possible injury risk reduction
- **Static stretching after training or on rest days** — not a performance or injury prevention tool, but acceptable if it feels good
- **Strength training as injury prevention** — the strongest evidence supports strength, not stretching
- **Address specific limitations** — ankle dorsiflexion and thoracic mobility are targeted because they affect running and lifting mechanics, not because "more flexibility is better"

### Key Reference

- Lauersen, J.B., Bertelsen, D.M., & Andersen, L.B. (2014). The effectiveness of exercise interventions to prevent sports injuries: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, 48(11), 871–877.
