A deload is a planned reduction in training stress designed to dissipate accumulated fatigue while preserving fitness. The physiological rationale is straightforward: fitness decays slowly, fatigue dissipates quickly. Exploiting this temporal asymmetry through planned deloads improves long-term training outcomes.

## The Fitness-Fatigue Model

Training produces two simultaneous effects:

1. **Fitness gain**: Slow, cumulative, decays over weeks
2. **Fatigue accumulation**: Faster, masks fitness expression, dissipates over days

After a training block, fatigue masks a portion of the fitness gained, you are fitter than you appear. A deload clears fatigue, revealing the underlying fitness. The result: performance improves despite (actually, because of) doing less work.

<ScientificNote>
The fitness-fatigue model was formalised by Banister et al. (1975) and has been refined through decades of training studies. The core insight (that fatigue and fitness operate on different time courses) explains why tapering before competition improves performance and why planned deloads improve long-term training outcomes. The model predicts that performance is approximately fitness minus fatigue; when fatigue is high, performance underestimates fitness.
</ScientificNote>

## What the Tapering Literature Shows

Tapering research (reducing training load before competition) provides the clearest evidence:

| Variable | Optimal Strategy |
|----------|-----------------|
| **Volume reduction** | 40–60% |
| **Intensity** | Maintain (do not drastically reduce) |
| **Frequency** | Maintain (do not skip sessions) |
| **Duration** | 1–2 weeks |
| **Performance improvement** | 2–5% (consistent across studies) |

These findings apply to regular deloads as well as pre-competition tapers. The mechanisms are the same: fatigue clearance while intensity maintains the neural and structural adaptations from training.

## Deload vs No Deload

No randomised controlled trial has directly compared "train continuously" to "periodically deload" over years, such a study would be impractical. However, the converging evidence from tapering research, overtraining studies, and practical coaching experience strongly supports planned deloads:

- Athletes who do not deload eventually deload involuntarily (injury or burnout)
- The 8–10 week timing reflects the typical timeline for significant fatigue accumulation at moderate training volumes
- Individual variation exists, some athletes need deloads at 6 weeks; others can sustain 12+

### Practical Implications for Atlas

- **Schedule deloads**: Every 8–10 weeks reduces decision-making burden
- **Reduce volume, not intensity**: Maintain the signal; reduce the stress
- **Do not skip sessions**: Frequency maintains the training habit and neuromuscular patterns
- **Running deload is separate**: Gym deload does not automatically require running deload
