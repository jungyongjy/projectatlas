Most supplements do not work. Of the ones that do, most provide marginal benefit that is irrelevant if the fundamentals — training, sleep, protein, calories — are not already in place.

Atlas recommends exactly two supplements. Both have strong evidence, favourable safety profiles, and meaningful benefit for a hybrid athlete.

## Recommended

### Whey Protein

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Purpose** | Convenient, fast-digesting protein to support daily intake targets |
| **Dose** | 1–2 scoops (25–50 g protein) daily |
| **Timing** | Morning (breakfast replacement) or post-training |
| **Evidence** | Strong — consistently shown to support muscle protein synthesis and recovery |

<InfoBox title="Whey is convenience, not magic">
Whey protein is not superior to chicken, eggs, or fish. It is more convenient. If your diet provides 140–160 g of protein from whole foods, whey is unnecessary. For most people with busy schedules, whey makes hitting the protein target practical.
</InfoBox>

### Creatine Monohydrate

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Purpose** | Increases phosphocreatine availability for short, high-intensity efforts; supports strength and power output; may modestly improve recovery |
| **Dose** | 5 g daily |
| **Timing** | Any time; consistency matters more than timing |
| **Loading phase** | Not necessary — 5 g daily achieves saturation in ~3–4 weeks |
| **Evidence** | Very strong — one of the most researched and consistently effective supplements available |

<ScientificNote>
Creatine monohydrate is the most extensively researched sports supplement in existence, with hundreds of studies demonstrating safety and efficacy. It improves performance in repeated high-intensity efforts (relevant to both interval running and resistance training), modestly increases lean mass through increased intracellular water retention and possibly enhanced training capacity, and may have neuroprotective properties. At 5 g daily, side effects are rare and limited to minor gastrointestinal discomfort in some individuals — typically resolved by taking with food.
</ScientificNote>

## Not Recommended

These supplements have insufficient evidence, unfavourable risk-to-benefit profiles, or are irrelevant to programme goals.

| Supplement | Why Not Recommended |
|------------|-------------------|
| **BCAAs** | Whey protein contains all BCAAs in adequate amounts. Isolated BCAAs provide no benefit beyond whole protein |
| **Pre-workouts** | Unnecessary stimulant load; performance benefit is from caffeine, which can be consumed as coffee |
| **Fat burners** | No credible evidence; potential safety concerns; irrelevant to programme goals |
| **Testosterone boosters** | No credible evidence for any over-the-counter product |
| **Glutamine** | No evidence for performance or recovery benefit in adequately fed athletes |
| **ZMA** | Only beneficial if zinc or magnesium deficient; supplementation beyond correcting deficiency shows no benefit |

<WarningBox title="The supplement industry is largely unregulated">
Many products contain ingredients not listed on the label. Some contain prohibited substances. The risk of contamination is real — particularly for products marketed for "testosterone support," "fat burning," or "extreme energy." Stick to products from reputable brands that undergo third-party testing.
</WarningBox>

## The Supplement Mindset

Supplements are the last 1–2% of performance. They cannot compensate for:

- Inadequate protein intake
- Poor sleep
- Inconsistent training
- Insufficient calories
- Chronic stress

<CoachNote>
Before considering any supplement beyond whey and creatine, ask: "Have I been sleeping 7–8 hours, eating 140–160 g of protein, and completing all my sessions for the past 4 weeks?" If the answer is no, supplements are not the solution.
</CoachNote>
