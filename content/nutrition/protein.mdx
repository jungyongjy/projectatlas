Protein is the most important macronutrient for a hybrid athlete. It repairs muscle tissue damaged during training, supports immune function, contributes to enzyme and hormone production, and is the most satiating macronutrient — helping regulate appetite naturally.

## Daily Target

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| **Daily intake** | 140–160 g |
| **Per meal (3 meals)** | ~40–50 g |
| **Per kg bodyweight** | ~1.8–2.0 g/kg |

<InfoBox title="Why 140–160 g?">
Current intake is estimated at 80–120 g/day — below the level that maximises muscle protein synthesis for an 80 kg athlete engaged in concurrent training. The target of 140–160 g (~1.8–2.0 g/kg) is supported by research on athletes combining endurance and resistance training, who have higher protein requirements than either modality alone.
</InfoBox>

## Why Protein Matters

| Role | Why It Matters for You |
|------|----------------------|
| **Muscle repair** | Training breaks down muscle; protein rebuilds it |
| **Muscle preservation** | Endurance training can be catabolic; adequate protein protects muscle mass |
| **Satiety** | Protein is the most filling macronutrient — helps regulate appetite |
| **Recovery** | Amino acids are the raw material for tissue repair |
| **Immune function** | Heavy training stresses the immune system; protein supports immune response |
| **Body composition** | Higher protein intake supports lean mass retention during energy deficit |

## Sources

### Primary Sources

| Source | Protein (approx.) | Notes |
|--------|------------------|-------|
| Chicken breast (150 g) | ~45 g | Lean, versatile, cost-effective |
| Whey protein (1 scoop) | ~25 g | Convenient, fast-digesting |
| Eggs (3 large) | ~18 g | Complete protein, nutrient-dense |
| Fish (150 g) | ~35–40 g | Omega-3 fatty acids + protein |
| Beef (150 g) | ~40 g | Iron, creatine, B12 |
| Greek yoghurt (200 g) | ~20 g | Convenient, calcium |

### Supplementary Sources

| Source | Protein (approx.) | Notes |
|--------|------------------|-------|
| Milk (250 ml) | ~8 g | With meals or post-training |
| Tofu/Tempeh (150 g) | ~15–20 g | Plant-based option |
| Lentils/Beans (1 cup) | ~15–18 g | Fibre + protein |
| Protein bar | ~15–25 g | Convenience; check sugar content |

<TipBox>
Spread protein intake across meals. The body can only use a certain amount of protein for muscle protein synthesis at one time (~30–50 g per meal). Consuming 140 g in two meals is less effective than spreading it across three to four eating occasions.
</TipBox>

## Practical Strategy

Current eating pattern:

| Meal | Current Protein (est.) | Target |
|------|----------------------|--------|
| Breakfast (protein shake) | ~25 g | ~25 g (adequate) |
| Lunch | ~30–40 g | ~40–50 g |
| Dinner | ~30–40 g | ~40–50 g |
| **Total** | **~85–105 g** | **~140–160 g** |

### How to Close the Gap

1. **Increase portions at lunch and dinner** — Add 50 g of chicken, fish, or meat to each meal (~15 g protein each)
2. **Add a second scoop of whey** — Morning or post-training shake with two scoops (~50 g)
3. **Greek yoghurt as dessert or snack** — 200 g adds ~20 g

<CoachNote>
The gap between current intake (~80–120 g) and target (~140–160 g) is approximately 40–60 g — roughly one additional chicken breast or two scoops of whey protein. This is not a dramatic dietary overhaul. It is a modest, sustainable increase.
</CoachNote>

## Common Mistakes

### Inadequate Total Intake

The most common error: simply not eating enough protein. Track intake for 3–5 days (estimate, do not weigh everything) to establish a baseline, then adjust.

### Uneven Distribution

All protein at dinner, very little during the day. Spreading intake across meals maximises muscle protein synthesis.

### Relying on Supplements

Whey protein is convenient but should supplement food, not replace it. Whole food protein sources provide additional nutrients (iron, zinc, B vitamins) that supplements lack.

<ScientificNote>
Protein requirements for athletes engaged in concurrent training are higher than for either endurance or strength athletes alone. Endurance training increases amino acid oxidation; strength training increases the threshold for muscle protein synthesis stimulation. The combination creates a higher total requirement — approximately 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day depending on training volume and energy balance.
</ScientificNote>
