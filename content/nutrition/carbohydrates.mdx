Carbohydrates are the primary fuel for moderate-to-high intensity running. Adequate carbohydrate intake supports training quality, recovery, and daily energy levels, without needing to count grams.

## Role of Carbohydrates

| Function | Why It Matters |
|----------|---------------|
| **Glycogen replenishment** | Muscles store carbohydrate as glycogen; running depletes these stores |
| **Training intensity** | Higher intensity running relies heavily on carbohydrate |
| **Recovery** | Post-training carbohydrate intake accelerates glycogen resynthesis |
| **Daily energy** | The brain and nervous system preferentially use glucose |
| **Protein sparing** | Adequate carbohydrate prevents the body from using protein for energy |

<InfoBox title="Carbohydrates are not the enemy">
Low-carbohydrate diets are incompatible with concurrent training goals. Running performance (especially at higher intensities) depends on carbohydrate availability. The goal is adequate intake, not avoidance.
</InfoBox>

## Daily Needs

Carbohydrate needs scale with training volume. For the current programme:

| Training Day Type | Carbohydrate Need |
|-------------------|-------------------|
| Rest day (Sunday) | Normal intake; no additional requirement |
| Easy run day (Thursday) | Moderate: slightly above baseline |
| Long run day (Saturday) | Moderate: slightly above baseline |
| Hard run day (Tuesday) | Higher: prioritise post-run replenishment |
| Gym day | Normal: strength training uses less glycogen than running |

<InfoBox title="No gram counting required">
Instead of tracking carbohydrate grams, focus on including a carbohydrate source with each meal, rice, pasta, bread, potatoes, oats, or fruit. Training appetite will naturally guide intake upward on days with higher demand.
</InfoBox>

## Practical Strategy

### Daily Sources

| Meal | Carbohydrate Source |
|------|-------------------|
| **Lunch** | Rice, noodles, bread, or potatoes |
| **Dinner** | Rice, pasta, potatoes, or bread |
| **Pre-run snack (if needed)** | Banana, toast, or small portion of oats |
| **Post-run** | Meal within 1–2 hours containing carbohydrates + protein |

### Around Training

| Timing | Recommendation |
|--------|---------------|
| **2–3 hours before running** | Normal meal containing carbohydrates |
| **30–60 minutes before (if needed)** | Small carbohydrate snack (banana, slice of toast) |
| **During easy runs (under 60 min)** | Water only: no intra-run fuel needed |
| **After running** | Carbohydrate + protein within 1–2 hours |

<TipBox>
For runs under 60 minutes at easy or moderate intensity, pre-existing glycogen stores are sufficient. You do not need gels, sports drinks, or intra-run carbohydrate. Water is adequate.
</TipBox>

## Common Mistakes

### Fear of Carbohydrates

Some athletes restrict carbohydrates due to weight gain concerns. This impairs running performance and recovery. Carbohydrates consumed around training are preferentially used for glycogen replenishment and energy, they are not stored as fat when energy balance is maintained.

### Inadequate Post-Run Carbohydrate

After a hard run (Tuesday intervals or 4×4), glycogen stores are partially depleted. Consuming carbohydrates within 1–2 hours accelerates replenishment. Delaying post-run eating impairs recovery and next-day performance.

### Overcomplicating Sources

Rice, pasta, bread, potatoes, oats, fruit. These are sufficient. You do not need specialised "sports nutrition" products for the training volumes in this programme.

<ScientificNote>
Glycogen resynthesis occurs at approximately 5–7% per hour under optimal conditions (adequate carbohydrate intake, rest). Full glycogen replenishment after significant depletion takes 20–24 hours. For an athlete training daily, consistent carbohydrate intake at each meal is more important than aggressive post-training supplementation, the cumulative effect of meals determines glycogen status, not any single feeding.
</ScientificNote>
