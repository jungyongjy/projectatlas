When you eat matters, but much less than what and how much you eat. Meal timing provides a modest performance and recovery benefit. It is the refinement layer, not the foundation.

## The Current Pattern

| Meal | Timing | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|
| Breakfast (protein shake) | Morning | Quick, adequate for non-training mornings |
| Lunch | Early afternoon | Largest meal |
| Dinner | Evening | Second main meal |

This pattern is simple and sustainable. The recommendations below are adjustments around training, not a restructuring of daily eating.

## Around Training

### Pre-Training

| Timing | Recommendation |
|--------|---------------|
| **2–3 hours before** | Normal meal containing protein + carbohydrates |
| **60 minutes before** | If hungry: small, easily digestible snack (banana, toast, small portion of oats) |
| **30 minutes before** | Water only (unless genuinely hungry) |

<InfoBox title="Training fasted vs fed">
For easy runs (Zone 2, under 45 minutes), training before breakfast is acceptable and may modestly enhance fat oxidation adaptation. For quality sessions (intervals, 4×4, threshold), training in a fed state supports the higher intensity required. The distinction between "easy" and "quality" sessions applies to nutrition timing as well as training structure.
</InfoBox>

### Post-Training

| Timing | Recommendation |
|--------|---------------|
| **Within 1–2 hours** | Meal containing protein + carbohydrates |
| **If next meal is >3 hours away** | Small snack (protein shake, Greek yoghurt, fruit) |

The "anabolic window" (the idea that protein must be consumed within 30 minutes of training) is overstated. Total daily protein intake is far more important than immediate post-training timing. However, consuming protein within 1–2 hours of training modestly improves recovery and should be the default practice.

<CoachNote>
Do not delay eating after a hard session because you are "not hungry." Appetite is often suppressed immediately after intense exercise. Eat anyway, even if it is a small amount. Your body needs the nutrients even if your appetite has not caught up.
</CoachNote>

## Meal Timing and Running

| Session Type | Pre-Run | Post-Run |
|-------------|---------|----------|
| **Zone 2 (Tue)** | Morning shake is sufficient | Normal lunch; no special timing needed |
| **Quality Run (Fri)** | Normal lunch 2–3 hours before afternoon run | Dinner within 1–2 hours of finishing |
| **Long Run (Sun)** | Breakfast (shake + banana or toast) 60–90 min before | Lunch within 1–2 hours of finishing |

## Meal Timing and Gym

| Session Timing | Pre-Gym | Post-Gym |
|---------------|---------|----------|
| **Lunchtime/Afternoon** | Normal meal 1–2 hours before | Next meal within 1–2 hours |
| **Evening** | Afternoon snack if needed | Dinner after session |

<TipBox>
The best meal timing schedule is the one that fits your life. A perfectly timed eating schedule that you cannot follow is worse than a "good enough" schedule that happens consistently. Fit nutrition around your day, not your day around nutrition.
</TipBox>

## Common Mistakes

### Overvaluing Timing Over Total Intake

An athlete who eats 140 g of protein at "suboptimal" times will outperform an athlete who eats 100 g of protein at "perfect" times. Total intake dominates timing. Get the quantity right first.

### Training on a Full Stomach

Eating a large meal immediately before training diverts blood flow to digestion and creates gastrointestinal discomfort. Allow 2–3 hours after a main meal before intense training.

### Skipping Post-Training Nutrition

"Not hungry" is not a reason to skip eating after a hard session. The body needs fuel even when appetite signals are suppressed.

<ScientificNote>
The post-exercise "anabolic window" is real but wider than commonly portrayed. Muscle protein synthesis remains elevated for 24–48 hours after resistance training and is responsive to protein intake throughout this period. The practical implication: total daily protein intake and consistent meal spacing matter more than rushing to consume protein immediately after training. The 30-minute "window" is a myth; the real window is hours, not minutes.
</ScientificNote>
